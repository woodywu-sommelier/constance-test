> If it's wine at a restaurant, you're entitled to flag it, and a good restaurant would rather you speak up than quietly drink something faulty.
>
> **Signs a bottle is actually faulty**
> - **Cork taint (TCA):** smells like wet cardboard, damp basement, or moldy newspaper, and the fruit tastes muted or flat.
> - **Oxidation:** tastes stale or flat, like bruised apple or sherry (when it's not supposed to). Whites may look deeper yellow or brown, and reds may look brownish.
> - **Heat damage:** tastes cooked, jammy, or stewed, and the cork may be pushed out slightly.
> - **Refermentation or spoilage:** unexpected fizz in a still wine, or a sharp vinegar or nail-polish-remover smell.
> - **Reduction:** smells like struck matches, rotten eggs, or burnt rubber. Sometimes this blows off with air, so give it a few minutes.
>
> **What to do**
> 1. **Give it a few minutes in the glass.** Some wines are closed up or funky at first and improve with air.
> 2. **Ask your server or the sommelier to taste it.** You can say, "This tastes a little off to me. Would you mind taking a look?" It's a normal request.
> 3. **Let them decide together with you.** If they agree it's flawed, they'll usually replace it without fuss. Many will also do so if you simply don't like it, especially if you ask politely, but they're not obligated to.
>
> **One caveat:** if the wine is clean but just not to your taste (more tannic, acidic, funky, or oaky than you expected), that isn't a fault, and a restaurant may not take it back. Even then, many will help you pick something else if you explain what you were hoping for.
>
> If you tell me what kind of wine it is and how it tastes or smells, I can help you figure out whether it's a flaw or just the style. And if you meant a different kind of bottle, let me know.
